UX proposal · Backdrop for macOS

Backdrop, RE-BLOCKED

re-block (v.) to restage a scene's moves without changing a word of the script.

Backdrop does two jobs in one window. A designer builds the show in daylight, and an operator runs it in the dark with the DSM's voice on cans. Today both jobs share the same three columns. This proposal gives each job a room of its own, puts the line the operator listens for at the centre of the screen, and lets the designer work on the stage itself instead of describing it with sliders.

Stays exactly as it is
Every function, and every key but one: Go to moves from the number keys to G. Space is still the only key that fires. The .backdrop file, BackdropCore, show control, Shortcuts, Spotlight, Quick Look and printing.
Gets re-imagined
The console's layout in both modes, the cue list, the Inspector, Add Cue, the morph and map windows, booth warnings, and crash recovery.

CUE0

Where it stands

Backdrop is careful, well-made software. Its rules about the keyboard and its checks on the booth Mac are better than most show tools. The interface around them is a tidy Mac form, and it treats the night of the show the same way it treats an afternoon of programming.

Keep these. They are the product.

  • The safety modelSpace is the only key that fires. Held keys never repeat, unhandled keys never beep, nothing in show mode takes the keyboard, show mode has no shortcut, and Shortcuts has no GO.
  • Standby and liveOnly GO puts a cue on stage. Every cue and every GO step carries its own cue point, the line or action to wait for.
  • The booth checksBackdrop watches mirroring, the main display, power, heat, Low Power Mode and where the show is stored, and says what to do about each.
  • Plain wordsMessages say what happened and what to do, in the operator's language. The redesign keeps every one of them word for word.
  • The renderersEvery effect already paints offscreen. That makes thumbnails, previews and animated cue cards cheap to add.

Where it rubs

  1. Two jobs, one layout.Show mode and edit mode share the same three columns. The operator's screen carries the designer's furniture, and the designer's screen carries the operator's key bar.
  2. The line to listen for is small.In show mode the cue point is 15 pt text in the right-hand column. It is the thing the operator is actually waiting for, yet the two previews take most of the window.
  3. GO is an ordinary button.It sits in the far corner at the size of Open Projector. Space is the real trigger, but the button is where the eye goes to check what Space will do.
  4. A white window in a dark booth.The console follows the Mac's appearance. On a light-mode Mac it is a large white panel in a blacked-out room, which costs the operator their night vision and can spill light into the house.
  5. Every warning looks the same."Another app is in front, so Space won't fire GO" and "This Mac is running on battery" are the same grey paragraph in the same box, and the box pushes the previews down.
  6. The running order is hard to read.Transitions are 10.5 pt rows such as "↓ fade 0.5 s", shown only in edit mode. A cue that continues by itself has a small arrow or clock at its right edge. Nothing shows which cues one GO will set running.
  7. Timing is abstract.Clock, hold, wave, pause, morph, layer and fade are sliders counted in "steps of the clock". Several need a paragraph of help; the one for Advance runs to 94 words.
  8. Fourteen effects, described in words.Add Cue lists Cascade, Decryption, Layered image and the rest with a one-line blurb. People need to see an effect to choose it.
  9. Spatial things are edited as numbers, or elsewhere.The projection area is four percentage sliders. Morph points and map routes open in separate windows.
  10. Problems are a single "!".A sheet issue is a brass exclamation mark on the row. There is no list of them and no reason on screen.
  11. Five keys get a permanent strip.The key bar takes 40 pt along the bottom in both modes, to explain five keys an operator learns in one rehearsal.

Principles for the redesign

  • Two jobs, two rooms

    Building a show and running one get layouts made for each.

  • Read at arm's length

    In the booth, the number, the line and GO are big enough to read at a glance from the script.

  • Draw the structure

    Transitions, continues and GO steps are drawn, so the order of events is visible.

  • Touch the stage

    Spatial and timed settings are edited where they show up, by dragging.

  • Safety is the floor

    Every rule that protects GO stays. Some get stronger.

CUE1

Two rooms

Show mode and edit mode stop being one layout with panels hidden. They become two rooms with different jobs, joined by the same cue list.

The Booth is for running a show. It is always dark, whatever the Mac's appearance, and it shows only what matters on the night, at a size that can be read from the script. The Studio is for building one. It follows the Mac's light or dark appearance, puts the stage in the middle, and lets the designer work on it directly.

Moving between them is a scene change. The panels cross-fade in about 300 ms while the cue list stays where it is, so it is always obvious which room you are in.

CUE2

The Booth

The operator has three places to look: the stage, the book, and this screen. The Booth is designed to be read in a second and then left alone.

The standby card is the hero. The next cue's number is set at 104 pt in brass, and its cue point at 33 pt in a typewriter face, as it reads in the prompt copy. The two monitors show On stage on the left in red and Next on the right in brass, with an arrow from one to the other carrying the transition GO will run. GO becomes a full-width plate with Space engraved on it.

  1. AStatus strip. While all is well it shows only the projector's lamp. Power, heat, disk and show control appear only when something needs attention.
  2. BOn stage, then Next. Equal monitors with red and brass tally borders, in the order the show runs, with the transition GO will run on the arrow between them.
  3. CThe standby card. Number, name and cue point, sized to read from the book. The cue point uses a typewriter face, as scripts do.
  4. DSteps. A cue that waits for GO partway shows its steps as diamonds, filled as they are taken.
  5. EThe GO plate. Brass to match the card, Space engraved on it, and a plain sentence on what it fires. Clicking it works as today; it never takes focus.
  6. FThe key row. A slim row under GO for the keys without a control of their own: the arrows, G to go to a cue, and B for blackout. Space is on GO, R sits by Run again, and ? shows them all.
  7. GThe rail. The cue list as a prompt book: number in a margin box, cue point in script type, a picture of each cue finished, and the joins between them. See Cue 3.
  8. HThe lock. Press and hold to leave the Booth.

Warnings, graded

The booth checks keep their exact wording. What changes is their weight. Anything that means GO won't reach the stage, or the audience may be looking at the console, is a stop: a red band across the top of the window that cannot be missed. Everything else is a caution: an amber lamp appears in the status strip with the message beneath it, and goes again once the problem clears. While all is well, the strip shows only the projector.

STOPAnother app is in front, so Space won't fire GO. Click this window to bring Backdrop back.
CAUTIONThis Mac is running on battery (64%). Plug it in: if the battery runs down, the Mac sleeps and the projector goes dark.
Stop: another app in front; the console's display mirrored; the Mac overheating badly enough that macOS is slowing it sharply.
Caution: battery; running hot; Low Power Mode; the show on an external drive or server; the projector on the main display, standing in, in a window, or disconnected; the OSC port taken.
CUE3

The prompt-book rail

The cue list becomes a drawing of the show's running order. Joins show how the stage changes between cues, links show which cues run on by themselves, and one GO's reach is visible before it is pressed.

Each row reads like a DSM's margin: the cue number boxed as it is pencilled in the book, the cue point in script type, and a picture of the cue finished, drawn by the same code that makes Finder's thumbnail. The live and next rows keep today's square tab down their left edge, with ON STAGE or NEXT reading up it. The rail is the same component in both rooms. In the Studio, a row's grip and delete appear on hover, and a + appears in the gap between rows.

Joins: how the stage changes

  • Snap. A hard edge: the cut is instant.
  • Fade through black. Out to black, then in. The standard.
  • Crossfade. One picture blends into the next.
  • Wipe. A slanted edge, angled from the side or corner it starts from.

Links: what happens after a cue

  • When cued. A broken line. Nothing happens until GO.
  • After a wait. A solid spine with the seconds on it.
  • When finished. A solid spine. The next cue fires when this one settles.

Marks

  • On stage in red, as a broadcast tally light.
  • Next in brass, the same brass as GO.
  • GO steps as diamonds under their cue, ticked once taken.
  • Issues as an amber tab and hatch, with the reason written on the row.

The sample show has no continues or issues, so cue 6's link and cue 7 "Harbour" are examples, marked as such. Clicking a join selects that cue's transition, as clicking a transition row does today.

CUE4

The Studio

The stage fills the middle, every timing setting becomes a bar you can drag, and anything spatial is edited on the picture itself.

Today's Inspector runs top to bottom: the cue, its content, then its settings. The Studio splits that by kind of decision. What belongs to the book (number, cue point, continue) and what the stage shows (text, pictures, key) stay in the Inspector. How it looks becomes a row of picture chips, so "Departures board" is a picture of a departures board. How it moves goes into the cue timeline under the stage.

  1. AToolbar. Lock for show, Add cue, Stage setup, the sheet's health, and the projector chip.
  2. BThe same rail as the Booth, with grip and delete on hover and a + between rows to add a cue right there.
  3. CThe stage. The selected cue, as large as the window allows, with handles wherever its kind has something spatial.
  4. DThe cue timeline. Hold, wave, pause, morph, layer, fade and move as bars on a ruler in seconds. The clock is the tempo at the left.
  5. EThe Inspector, regrouped: In the book, On stage, Look. Choices with a visual result become picture chips.
  6. FRun again plays the cue and sweeps a playhead along the timeline, so you can see which bar is which.

The timeline for a cue with steps

Cue 5 keys in 4417, waits for GO, then presses OK and flashes the boxes green. On the timeline the key presses sit a pause apart, and the GO step is a diamond. Everything after a diamond waits for the operator. Dragging a pause gap changes Pause for the whole cue, as the slider does today. Timeline drags merge into one undo step, as slider drags already do.

CUE5

The cue gallery

Add Cue becomes a gallery of the fourteen effects, each card playing a few seconds of its own effect, drawn by its own renderer.

The search, the keywords and Return-picks-the-first all stay: "safe" still finds Keypad and "hacker" still finds Decryption and Cascade. Cards are grouped by what they put on stage, and the sheet says where the new cue will go. Previews play only while visible, and stand still under Reduce Motion.

CUE6

Beginners and recovery

Two moments decide whether an operator trusts the software: the minutes before the house opens, and the seconds after something goes wrong.

Beginners, named for the call that sends actors to their opening places, is what the Booth shows until the first GO. It gathers every check Backdrop already makes into one list, green or amber, with the three things macOS won't let Backdrop check written underneath as reminders. Nothing new is stored. After the first GO it goes away, and from then on only problems show, in the status strip.

Recovery replaces the alert shown after a crash. It offers the same two choices, Resume at Cue 4 and Start from the Top, in the Booth's own look, with the picture of cue 4 and the time it was fired. Both come from the crash record Backdrop already writes, so the operator can check the book before choosing.

CUE9

Building it

The whole proposal sits in the app target. BackdropCore, the show file and the recorded test outputs stay as they are.

Where the work lands

  • Console/ConsoleWindow.swiftIts one layoutContent() becomes a Booth layout and a Studio layout that share the rail and the monitors, with a cross-fade between them.
  • Console/Desk.swiftGains a fixed Booth palette, plus a high-contrast version for Increase Contrast. The system palette stays for the Studio.
  • CueList/CueRowView.swift, TransitionRowView.swift, SubCueRowView.swiftMerge into the rail: margin boxes, thumbnails, joins, continue links and issue hatching.
  • Panels/StandbyPanel.swift, HeaderView.swift, KeyBar.swift, WarningBox.swiftBecome the standby card, the GO plate, the status strip and the warning tiers. KeyBar is retired.
  • Editing/CueEditorView.swift, SettingsForms.swiftRegroup into In the book, On stage and Look. Timed SettingKeys move to a new timeline view that reads the *CuePlan types.
  • Editors/The morph points and route views move into the stage as focus modes; their models are reused as they are.
  • App/ShowKeys.swiftStops taking digits, the point, Return, Escape and Delete, and takes G to open the Go To panel. The panel calls the existing standBy(number:), which already handles letters and says when no cue has that number. The keypad entry in Playhead goes unused and can be removed later, with its tests. The README's key table changes to match.
  • Debug/Snapshots.swiftLearns the new states, so -BackdropSnapshot keeps covering every screen.

In four phases

  1. The BoothStandby card, GO plate, monitors, status strip, warning tiers, the Go To panel, Beginners and Recovery. It is where the night of the show gains the most, so it goes first.
  2. The railMargin boxes, thumbnails, joins, links, steps and issues. It ships into both modes and makes the running order readable.
  3. The cue gallerySelf-contained: one sheet and a small offscreen preview loop per kind.
  4. The StudioThe timeline, Look chips, Stage setup on the canvas, then morph and map focus modes. The largest phase, and it can land one piece at a time.

Risks to weigh

A fixed dark look in show modeIt departs from following the Mac's appearance. The argument is the booth itself, and Increase Contrast still gets a high-contrast palette.
RelearningExisting operators will find things in new places. The keys don't change, so muscle memory holds where it matters.
A truthful timelineSome timings depend on content: letter counts, layers, stops. The timeline has to draw from the cue plans, never from estimates.
Custom drawingThe rail and the timeline are drawn by hand, like today's rows. They need the same care with VoiceOver labels and focus that the rows get now.
Preview costFourteen animated cards could load the GPU. Draw only visible cards, at small size, and stop them under Reduce Motion.
